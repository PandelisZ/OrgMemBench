Maya Patel and Luis Hernandez launched Helix Logistics in a cramped co‑working space in early 2020, armed with a lean stack and a handful of friends who believed in the idea of a cloud‑first freight‑forwarding platform. The first hires were Ethan Kim, a backend engineer with a knack for Go, and Sofia Martinez, a UX designer who had spent the previous year building dashboards for a logistics startup. Ravi Patel joined as a sales operations specialist, handling the first inbound leads and learning the ropes of HubSpot, which the founders had just adopted to keep track of prospects. The team built a minimal viable product in three months, focusing on a simple shipment tracking interface that pulled data from carriers’ legacy systems via CSV uploads. The MVP landed on a Friday, and the next day the founders received a call from River Freight, a regional carrier that had been struggling with manual spreadsheets. River agreed to a one‑month pilot, hoping Helix could reduce their reconciliation time.

The pilot was a mixed bag. On the first week, the platform worked flawlessly, and River’s team praised the clean UI and real‑time status updates. But during the peak season in late November, a misconfigured load balancer caused a near‑miss outage that left several shipments in limbo for a few hours. Ethan and Sofia worked through the night, rolling back the last deployment and patching the routing rules. The incident was documented in a hastily written Confluence page titled “November 2020 Outage – Root Cause and Fix,” but the page was never updated with a post‑mortem or a formal incident response playbook. The team realized that their informal documentation was insufficient, and they began to experiment with Linear for engineering tickets, Otter.ai for meeting transcripts, and Figma for design iterations, but the culture of disciplined documentation was still in its infancy.

Strategically, the founders decided to keep the product simple and focus on the pilot, rejecting the idea of building a full API integration at launch. They also chose to keep the data warehouse in Snowflake, but only for raw logs, not for analytics, because they were still figuring out what metrics mattered. The decision to use HubSpot for CRM was solidified, and the team began to use GitHub for source control, but the codebase was still a handful of scripts and a few microservices. The year ended with the MVP validated, the first pilot secured, and a clear sense that the next step would be to build a more robust, scalable architecture that could handle the demands of a growing customer base.
