# Postmortem: Mis‑configured Load Balancer Outage — 2023-10-20

**Incident date:** 2023-10-12  
**Severity:** SEV-2  
**Authors:** Ethan Brooks (DevOps Engineer), Daniel Kim (Platform Engineer)  
**Status:** Final  

## Summary

On 12 Oct 2023 a 3‑hour outage of our public API was caused by a mis‑configured load balancer that failed to route traffic correctly. The outage impacted all mid‑market freight‑forwarding customers using our API for shipment tracking and rate calculations. The incident was detected by our monitoring system, escalated to the on‑call team, and resolved after traffic was rerouted and the load balancer was re‑configured.

## Timeline

- **09:00** – Monitoring alerts triggered: API latency spiked, error rate > 5 %.  
- **09:05** – On‑call engineer (Ethan) acknowledged alert, began diagnostics.  
- **09:15** – Incident escalated to platform team; Daniel joined.  
- **09:30** – Initial hypothesis: load balancer mis‑routing.  
- **10:00** – Confirmed mis‑configuration in the load balancer’s backend pool.  
- **10:15** – Traffic manually rerouted to healthy backend instances.  
- **10:30** – Load balancer re‑configured with correct backend pool.  
- **11:00** – API metrics returned to normal; error rate dropped to 0 %.  
- **12:00** – Incident closed; post‑mortem drafted.  

## Root cause

The root cause was a mis‑configured load balancer that was pointing to an outdated backend pool. The mis‑configuration caused the load balancer to route all traffic to a set of instances that were not serving the API, leading to a complete service outage. The mis‑configuration was introduced during a recent deployment of the load‑balancing layer, but no automated health checks or configuration validation were in place to catch the error before it impacted production.

## Contributing factors

- Lack of automated health checks on the load balancer configuration.  
- No configuration validation pipeline to detect incorrect backend pools.  
- Absence of a monitoring alert for mis‑configurations in the load balancer.  
- Limited visibility into the load balancer’s state during the incident.  

## What went well

- Rapid detection through existing latency and error‑rate alerts.  
- Clear communication between on‑call and platform teams.  
- Quick identification of the mis‑configuration and manual traffic rerouting.  
- Thorough documentation of the incident and root cause.  

## What went poorly

- The mis‑configuration was not caught by automated checks.  
- No alert existed for load balancer mis‑configurations, delaying detection.  
- The incident lasted 3 hours, impacting customers more than necessary.
